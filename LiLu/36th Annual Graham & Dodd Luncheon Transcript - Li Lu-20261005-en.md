### The Six Core Principles of Value Investing

**Tano Santos:** I’m going to talk for about 15 minutes, and then I’ll open it up for questions. I’m going to ask you to keep them short and to the point, and I will be ruthless. If you take more time than I consider necessary to get your point across, I’ll cut you off and give you a nasty look. Keep them short, because I’m sure there will be many questions.

Li Lu, I want to start here. As I said, you are one of the great names in modern value investing. You’re deeply intertwined with the story of value investing at Columbia Business School. We were talking about that over lunch. You were there at the very beginning when Bruce was launching this program. We know of your friendship and partnership with Charlie and Warren, and how many things you guys did together.

We will be celebrating 30 years of the founding of Himalaya Capital next year, which is a remarkable achievement. I want to start here. I want you to tell us a little bit: What have you learned about value investing, and where do you think we are today? What is the thing you wish you had known at the beginning? What is the lesson we have now that we didn’t have when you were studying these ideas at Columbia Business School?

**Li Lu:** Thank you, Tano. First of all, thank you all for being here. It is such a wonderful occasion.

Back when I was a student, as well as a young professional, I also attended a few of those luncheons. It was the highlight. I hope I do some justice now, 30 years later.

The discipline of value investing started with Columbia. When Ben Graham first started his classes, it was almost 90 years ago. Of course, he practiced 10 or 20 years before that, so we call it a 100-year-old discipline. It really has evolved, but some of the core principles have not and have become timeless.

If I have to distill the main principles for the discipline of value investing, I would say there are six in my mind. Three come directly from Ben Graham, the namesake of today’s lunch; one each from Warren and Charlie; and we add a small twist — you could call that the sixth.

Here are the six basic principles in my mind that have guided our discipline, at least our practice, over the last 33 years:

1. **A stock is a piece of ownership:** Ben Graham laid it out pretty quickly as he started this discipline with three basic ideas. A stock is a piece of ownership, not a piece of paper to trade around. Buying stock is really the same as buying a company.
2. **Mr. Market is there to serve you, not instruct you:** Secondly, the market is there to serve you, not to instruct you, which is a profound insight if you are in the midst of market activities.
3. **Margin of safety:** Thirdly, investing is about predicting the future, which is inherently unpredictable. Therefore, you always want to require a margin of safety that will help protect the downside as well as make a better upside.
4. **Circle of competence:** When Warren practiced, I think he added this further discipline, which I call a circle of competence. All of us are probably predisposed to have certain interests, and over time you develop particular expertise and try to study more and make it better. The most important concept of a circle of competence is knowing the edge of it, because most of us are wired to think we know a little more than we actually do. Understanding the edge is as important as understanding what is inside it.
5. **Fish where the fish are:** Charlie had this idea that you’ve got to fish where the fish are. He was a lifelong fisherman, and that is a wonderful analogy for the actual market. There are certain markets, industries, and businesses that draw a lot of attention. With that competition, your returns are averaged out. Then there are other industries, businesses, countries, or markets that don’t attract enough attention and have far fewer fishermen. You do way better to fish where the fish are. It’s a simple idea, but in practice it is amazingly effective.
6. **Wealth as percentage ownership of global purchasing power:** Lastly, over my own 33 years of practice as a global investor, applying the principles of value investing on a global basis at a time when global commerce is becoming one single market. One insight we have is that wealth, in the end, is really the percentage ownership of global purchasing power. It isn’t expressed, especially in the modern economy, in the amount of gold, diamonds, or currencies of any kind. With inflation, all of those will dissipate over time. What is important is what you can buy with it, what you can use it for. A better measurement of wealth is your percentage ownership of global purchasing power.

The way we express that is by buying great businesses in the most dynamic economies at advantageous prices, waiting long enough for value to be created over time, and sharing proportionally in the upside. Here are the six basic ideas that I think can withstand the test of time and will serve everyone well.

**Tano Santos:** I like this last point very much. I’m already deviating from the script you and I agreed on, because I think going forward, keeping an eye on that purchasing power is going to be key if we’re not able to control inflation in the presence of these fiscal challenges. If we have time, we’ll come back to this.

**Li Lu:** Sure.

### The Four Market Participants & Society's Church vs. Casino

**Tano Santos:** I want to get a question in before we run out of time, for the benefit of my students in the room. This is my prerogative as the person asking you the questions, so we’ll get this out of the way.

Value investing as a practice has seen ups and downs. It’s been improving conceptually over time, with Bruce, your leadership, and many of the people in this room as well. What is the future of the equity analyst in the age of AI? Do you think we can still teach what we do at Columbia Business School going forward?

**Li Lu:** Let me answer that in two parts. First, what investors do, and then let’s talk about AI separately.

If you look at market participants, there are basically four different kinds of people:
1. **Investors** who stay with the company over time for value creation.
2. **Arbitrageurs** who arbitrage the rights and wrongs of market pricing. In Ben Graham’s terms, as a realist, you buy from the pessimist and sell to the optimist. Those are the arbitrageurs.
3. **Speculators** who are in it mostly for speculation, mostly guessing what other people would buy or sell in a certain security. They don’t care about either the value pricing of the security or value creation.
4. **Brokers and Market Makers** who facilitate all that.

Most people are not necessarily purists. They encompass somewhat of all four. If you have indexing, it is exactly that average, in a sense.

But value investors are into getting the right pricing discovery and waiting for value creation over a period of time. I would say Ben Graham was more of an arbitrageur, and Warren is using the arbitrage price, but mostly for value creation.

The social benefit of all different ones is also quite interesting. Speculators, by definition, don’t stay there long enough for any of the value changes or creations, so they add up to roughly zero. Whatever is made and lost probably adds up exactly to that. Net to society, it is a cost to the functioning of the market.

Brokerage and market making are also a cost to the functioning of the market. The market exists primarily for the goal of channeling excess savings into productive use, into deserving companies, and creating this modern economy that has compound economic growth. That is why the stock market exists in the first place.

Why do you have this speculative side? It’s part of the human condition. Warren always describes the stock market as a church with a casino attached to it. The church is where the values are created, and the casino is almost part of the building permit, a necessary cost because of the human condition. Four hundred years ago, when it was created, this was how you inspired the animal spirit, and 400 years later it is still doing that.

This is where you’re going to make a choice as students. Do you want to be in the zero-sum game, or the value creation game? That’s a personal choice. As a society, you also have a choice. You don’t want the casino side to be so out of proportion that it overwhelms the primary objective of why the market exists in the first place.

Those are choices on the individual level as well as for society. Over 400 years, despite all this huge boom and bust, giant waste, somehow this mechanism has survived and continued to propel society as a core function. It is pretty miraculous, to say the least.

For your students, it is important to make that distinction early on: What do you want to do personally? As a society, we should also calibrate the proportion of each. But they coexist as part of the human condition.

### Generative AI: Tool vs. Creature, Economics, and the Frontier

**Li Lu:** Now, onto AI and the market. That’s a more complex question. First of all, what is AI, or this wave of generative AI?

It is obviously a technology, a huge technological wave. As a technology, we’ve been dealing with that for the last few hundred years, ever since the Industrial Revolution. We have wave after wave of technological change, and the disruption and creation associated with it. Through that process of creative destruction, we understand some aspects of it. But somehow we feel this wave is different. Every wave is different, but this wave is unusually different from anything we’ve experienced so far.

What makes it so different this time? On the surface, you can see the pace of change, the scale involved, and the concentrated financial impact at the top, the nature of that concentrated financial impact at the top. They’re all incredibly unprecedented.

Then you ask why that is the case. The narrative is slightly different. Most of the time, until this moment, we’ve been dealing with technological changes as a fundamental tool for human productivity.

With this technology, we introduce a different element. We now have a spectrum of tool versus creature, and we begin to change on that spectrum of tool versus creature. The moment it was first introduced, nearly four years ago, was the moment of the Turing test. At that point, we could no longer tell from language whether it was a machine or a human.

From that point on, we were introduced to reasoning, and it began to gain more and more human features. From reasoning comes coding. With coding capability, it becomes an autonomously executing agent that functions much as a human does. Then comes the collaboration of all the agents in complex and long-lasting tasks—call that long reasoning.

Much like human society, agents would organize, self-organize, prioritize, and execute tasks in an ever more sophisticated fashion, with their own language communication tools. That’s where we are.

If you look at the Silicon Valley narrative, the generative AI will go to the stage of RSI, which we’re partially in – the idea that all the improvement would eventually be self-made, without a human in the loop. From RSI we will come to AGI, then ASI, the intelligence level exceeding all humans, individually as well as together. From there, you would say we’re creating somewhat of a semi-god or demigod, but a man-made creation.

Because that is so powerful and all-encompassing, the economic impact, by definition, has to be huge and therefore justify all the different investment costs. When you have trillion-dollar IPOs, you’re all invited to participate in the IPO of a god or something.

That is the narrative, and that is what makes it different. But if you step back and say – If this were indeed the case, what would become of humans? We would live in a society lorded over by a man-made god, accessed by very few and understood by none. Not even controlled – we can’t say that, because nobody understands it at that point.

Is there anyone in humanity who would like to live in that society? My answer is probably a resounding no. I don’t think anyone would want to live in that society.

There are only two outcomes, in a sense:
- **A)** This technology is not progressing in that direction, and we can discuss what it is and why it is not; or
- **B)** If it is indeed progressing in that direction, we will all, at some point, unite together to stop it from becoming that.

In neither case are we going to have a man-made god living and lording over human society.

That is what distinguishes this generation of technology, why it is different, and the different impacts, particularly the financial market impact. But leaving that aside, when you ask – Is this a useful technology? The answer is also a resounding yes.

What is it, in essence? If I were to give it a provisional definition, from observing what it does and where it came from – Generative AI is a machine that by applying brute compute with clever algorithms, using all human-generated data and its first derivative, come up and establish systematic contextual correlations among near-infinite variables of human-generated data and its first derivative. In doing so, it establishes capabilities that include much of human reasoning and human intelligence. At present, we know how it works and how to make it better, but we don’t quite understand why it works, much as to the extent that we don’t quite understand how human machinery works.

With that provisional definition, observing what is happening now, it equalizes a layer of the economy that is very helpful. If you look at the economy:
- At the bottom we have **energy and transportation**.
- On top of that sits **manufacturing**.
- On top of that, we have **communication and digital media**.
- Then we have an enormous amount of **knowledge-based, technology-based services** that synthesize all the knowledge we’ve accumulated and serve all the different layers of the economy.

Generative AI today, at its current stage, is great at synthesizing and equalizing all the knowledge and expertise that has accrued to that layer, helping all the other ones. But it can’t change, for example, energy and transportation. It doesn’t change manufacturing, which is essentially the combination of different molecules and atoms and transforming them into different combinations.

In communication and digitization, they’re already great synthesizers. That is the realm where it is going. It has not gone beyond humans. By this extension, it may not rise to whatever the narrative would dictate or lead you to believe. We’re still firmly within the human-generated data realm.

To go to the biological sense requires a whole different level. For example, our—this may be going too far.

**Tano Santos:** Go for it. Knock yourself out.

**Li Lu:** Each cell contains let’s say at least fifty petabytes of files, and you need to multiply that by trillions to get us on the biological level. We’re not anywhere close to getting to that level.

But what has been established already begins to tip this balance between tool versus creature. One of the two arguments would persuade you to stop any further development without a human in the loop.

RSI – recursive self-improvement – without a human in the loop would be very dangerous. They would develop goals and subgoals for their own purposes, and that would get them further from human control. [Who defines the “I” in RSI, humans or machines alone, that seems to me is a redline. Crossing it, we may not be able to get back.]

In summary, this technology is enormously helpful, transformative, and extraordinarily dangerous. We have to, as a society and together as human beings, have a red line to firmly establish that whatever the self-learning or utilization of the tools, it cannot be without a human in the loop. In other words, firmly establish it on the tool side rather than the creature side.

It’s also enormously disruptive, from employment to emotional dependency to risks from cyber to bio to nuclear. In the end, what you want society to develop is a balanced pace of development that balances benefit and risk.

By the way, this technology will always have incremental costs. This is fundamentally different from the internet or a bunch of previous technologies, where you have a one-time investment and can amortize it for a long time. This is not the case. You always have an incremental cost associated with it.

Cost, revenue, and profit are also very important. Capital needs and financial discipline will always be in operation. Also, the pace of development and the level of societal tolerance. You have four or five different layers of considerations for pacing and development into it.

How does human society adapt to those kinds of changes? Not always by planning. We’re going to go through big boom and bust cycles, or even multiple ones. That is the nature of how it is going to develop. Those are the lines we have to think about as we approach this transformative, dangerous, disruptive, and costly technological wave.

**Tano Santos:** I think what you said, Li Lu, about this combination of being extraordinary, dangerous, and our not completely understanding why it is generating some of the answers it is generating, would require a human in the loop to check over it.

**Li Lu:** Right.

**Tano Santos:** Once again, it’s going to be the scarce supply of this skill, of the person monitoring the artificial intelligence output, that will determine where rents are going to be located.

**Li Lu:** That’s right.

**Tano Santos:** That effectively means if you’re smart and can communicate effectively with that AI inside any domain, you’re going to be the scarce resource, and probably the rents are going to accrue to you. Do I understand this correctly?

**Li Lu:** I think that’s somewhere along those lines. It has different aspects and nuances, but I think that’s the case in the end.

### Market Valuation Extremes & Rationality in Tech Waves

**Tano Santos:** Let’s start projecting these ideas. One of the things that is most striking when we walk into the classroom—there are many of my colleagues here teaching capital markets, financial markets, value investing, investing in general—is that this is one peculiar market.

I have a very vague question for you. The market seems to be driven by a very low number of companies that are pushing the technological frontier. The world seems dependent on these companies to push this frontier, and as a result, valuations are completely distorted by that.

There’s a plot I show my students: the market capitalization of the company with the highest market capitalization, normalized by GDP. That number traditionally has been between 2 and 4 percent. It is now about 15 percent with Nvidia.

How do you think about this market, one that is becoming overly dependent on those few companies, with a very high beta to those companies? How do we design portfolios to protect ourselves against the shocks of this technology?

**Li Lu:** The market always does peculiar things.

**Tano Santos:** Yes.

**Li Lu:** From the very beginning, we go through this, and we’ll probably continue to do so. You don’t have to be dictated to by what the market decides to do at any given time. This is one of the most important insights and contributions from Ben Graham himself.

At this point, if you look at the proposition of one recent prospectus that I read. Here’s a company that calls itself a research lab, yet it burns through tens of billions and promises to continue burning through hundreds of billions of dollars. It promises a transformative technology, but at the same time has a 10 percent chance of killing everyone. It wrote that in the prospectus and invited everybody to put in at least $100 billion at a $2 trillion valuation.

If there is any inconsistency or irony?! Then this is. This is one extreme illustration of how irrational the market gets. It cannot both be true, but that’s where they are. It also tells you that things are not rational and not sustainable. Eventually, they won’t continue. Those are also the rules of the market, if you look throughout the centuries.

They do strange things over a long period of time. Take the S&P since 2010, for example. In the last 16 years, it averaged 14 or 15 percent [of annualized return]. In the preceding 10 years, from 2000 to 2010, it averaged exactly zero — in fact, slightly negative for 10 years.

If you average the last 26 years, you get high single digits, and that is roughly right over the long term. If GDP grew in that period maybe 2 percent, and you have 2 to 3 percent inflation, you average about 4 to 5 percent nominal growth.

Publicly listed companies do slightly better, especially those chosen by elimination to be the 500 best companies. You come up with high single digits, and that is where the long-term average has been.

That should serve as a basic grounding pillar of getting what you deserve and how the market rationalizes over a longer period. But it is punctuated by periods, sometimes long periods, when it goes to extremes.

The example we just cited does strike people as, at a minimum, odd. That happens throughout history. If we ground society in the sense that a super-creature is simply not allowed or tolerated, it won’t go there.

If it stays in the realm of tools, it will be like any other tool, with costs and benefits associated with it. Therefore, financial discipline and capital market discipline will eventually apply. The way you get there is through multiple ups and downs and boom and bust cycles, and that’s also fine.

From that perspective, the bifurcation as a result of it — at any given time, you don’t create an enormous amount of new capital. If it is overwhelmingly driven to the top, that means you also have a whole bunch of otherwise wonderful businesses trading at very low prices. This is the same here, in China, or anywhere else. In China, it is probably more extreme, as some of the Chinese participants [in the room] would be well aware.

Over time, the weight of the market will dictate the long-term returns of investors. You still get what you deserve in the end. There’s not much of a free lunch, even if it appears to be for a period of time, even a long period. Over the longer term, it will be weighed out by the natural weight of market rationalization itself. That’s why it has existed for a few hundred years already.

I would say this bifurcation offers those with a rational mind a better opportunity set than at other times, if you stay rational, which is very difficult. It’s easy to get swept up, especially when you have a big technology wave. Your margin of imagination can expand dramatically.

The speculative side of the market gets much more active, and the imagination becomes wilder, so the exaggerations tend to be more extreme. But for a rational mind, those create a better long-term opportunity set than otherwise.

### China's Industrial Transition: Objectives vs. Costs

**Tano Santos:** You mentioned China briefly there. Let’s talk a little bit about that. I just came back from China. I was teaching the participants in Hong Kong, and I took advantage of that occasion to do a couple of cases on Chinese companies and study the Chinese market a little.

It’s been quite a ride over the last few years. How do you think about the Chinese market these days, with the problems in the Chinese economy associated with the real estate bust and the banking crisis? It looks reminiscent of some of the things we’ve seen in other places. How do you see investing in China these days?

**Li Lu:** You mentioned the Chinese market. If you look at the last 10 years, it has basically been stagnant. What happened in those 10 years is fascinating.

Ten years ago, the Chinese government had a decision to make. They had achieved somewhat of a middle-income level. The question was: How do we break away from it? So many countries have reached that level and stagnated there, so much so that there is a term to describe it – the middle-income trap. Dozens of countries achieved that and stayed there 40 or 50 years later.

China is a large economy, so the question naturally is: What keeps a middle-income country stagnant and prevents it from breaking through the ceiling? You manufacture, you industrialize, you go with mid- to low-end manufacturing, but never break away from it.

They had a decision to make. Do we want to break out, and can we do something to accelerate that process or make sure it happens? On its natural course, a lot of countries have failed to achieve that and stayed there for 40 or 50 years, some countries even longer.

They embarked on this process of deliberately migrating the economy from low- to mid-end manufacturing into mid- to high-end manufacturing. Instead of a five-year plan, they had a 10-year plan called Made in China 2025, in which they listed about 10 major objectives aimed at transforming Chinese basic manufacturing from low- to mid-end into mid- to high-end.

A few years after that started, the first Trump administration came on board and started the trade war. This reinforced their idea that not only might they not break through that ceiling on their own natural course, but other countries might deliberately constrain them underneath it.

The first Trump administration was followed by the Biden administration, which not only kept the trade tariffs but also added more technology restrictions. Five years into the 10-year plan, by 2021, the Chinese government doubled down on that direction.

In addition to migrating manufacturing into the mid- to high-end, it added technological self-sufficiency, particularly in areas where they had real bottlenecks: semiconductors, big aircraft engines, operating software code, and so on. There was a long list China was facing. That started another five years of doubling down and accelerating that process.

When you try to achieve those impossible goals that so many countries have failed to achieve, it doesn’t come without deep and heavy costs. That has been the model by which China developed its economy. At different periods, they have big objectives and targets, with big costs associated with them.

You mentioned real estate. In the last five years, one of the principal costs of achieving that goal has been the balance sheets and family wealth of middle-class Chinese families. By 2021, real estate prices reached their peak. A typical middle-class Chinese family had 70 percent of its net worth tied up in the home it owned. But that home carried roughly 50 percent leverage through a mortgage.

In the next five years, until now, real estate prices nationwide lost about 40 percent of their value on average. They saw their entire family fortune go down 60 to 70 percent on average.

When that happens, what do you do? You do three things: consume less, save more, and, to the extent possible, pay down debt. That is exactly what typical middle-class Chinese families have done over the last five years.

Domestic demand has been growing slower and slower, to today roughly zero. Savings have gone up to about 120 percent of GDP, probably the largest savings deposits anywhere in the world, basically RMB 165 trillion.

People have also been through two or three years of roughly zero price increases or slightly negative ones, more or less in that range. Therefore, they have a bit of a confidence issue when they look to the future.

That is one tier of the economy. But if you look at the other tier, at the Chinese government’s objectives over the last 10 years and five years, you see a remarkably different picture. Chinese manufacturing has finally broken through from low- to mid-end, firmly into mid- to high-end.

Every single objective of Made in China 2025 has been hit. In the last five years, solving the bottleneck issues has also made tremendous progress. Take the example of semiconductors. It turns out that the U.S.-led technology sanctions have been a godsend for the Chinese semiconductor industry because they have one of the most precious things in a market economy – DEMAND.

Ask any entrepreneur what is most important, and they say the order book. If you have revenue, you get the whole ball rolling. With more revenue comes profit, comes R&D, comes better improvement, comes more revenue. That happened to the point that, on mature nodes, both the Chinese equipment side and fabrication side have achieved almost complete self-sufficiency. On more cutting-edge, advanced nodes, they are getting close to self-sufficiency, with a few key missing ingredients.

If you keep this way, on its natural course it will happen. That’s just one aspect. One of the key capabilities of generative AI is producing intelligence at scale on demand. One of the biggest applications is code generation, and that solves a big problem in some of those bottlenecks, primarily the missing layer of operating code systems.

From PCs to servers to semiconductors to CUDA system of Nvidia, one by one, all of those are now being replaced with the help of AI. This becomes a great equalizer.

Field after field, over the last 10 years, you’ve seen remarkable progress in migrating the basic contours of manufacturing from low- to mid-end to mid- to high-end. In some areas—new energy, EVs, storage, batteries, you name it, and it goes on and on, heavy machinery—they dominate. They have become global leaders. In areas where they have bottlenecks, they have also begun to migrate and close the gap.

At the same time, it comes with very heavy costs to other industries. Real estate, for example, on an annual new sales basis, we’re now probably down about 70 percent. That RMB 10 trillion [in declining sales] is also exactly how much has been spent on manufacturing upgrading and technology efficiency, about RMB 10 trillion a year. If you look at the diversion, it is around 2021.

That has been the development model if you look back over the last 50 years of China’s development. At any given time, roughly every 10 years or so in my mind, you get a new set of objectives and targets, and it comes with heavy costs.

Sometimes in the next 10 years, the objective could become the cost. In the early days of industrial takeoff, the environment, air pollution, and rivers all became costs. In the proceeding next 10 years, they became the objectives, and new environmentally friendly technologies were developed. The polluters were the cost.

In the course of urbanization, real estate developers were the objective, building China into what it is, with home ownership and firm middle-class wealth. In the last five or 10 years, they became the cost.

You begin to suffer quite a bit, but overall you see the progress of the country through those periods. That is why you can compress development over a few hundred years into, say, 40 years. But at any given time, as an investor or entrepreneur — and I’m sure all those people from China would strongly identify — you always want to know whether you are the objective or the cost.

Being the cost of a state objective is no fun! I’m sure all of you intimately know that or have friends involved in the process.

That’s how it has developed. Overall, if you give the score, you would say that, of all those changes, you now have a country of such size and population that has finally embarked on a complete course of modernization. Meanwhile, you have some of the greatest companies, globally competitive, trading at very low prices. Again, you have the bifurcation you see here. If you’re a rational investor and look at the whole picture, you might take a different perspective.

I would say that in today’s market, you probably find a better opportunity set than you would at different times. That’s what you want.

### The Global Common Market, Strategic Stability, and Mutual Dependency

**Tano Santos:** I’m going to take questions in a moment. While you get your questions ready, raise your hand. I want to ask you one last question, very quickly.

You founded Himalaya Capital in 1997. I arrived at the University of Chicago about the same time as a young assistant professor. The world, at least to me — I don’t know how it looked to you then; we didn’t know each other — was very different, or looked very different from how it looks to me now.

It was a world that was open, transnational, where with travel you could do so many things. It had, I don’t know how to say it, a generosity of spirit that seems to be a little lacking today. It looks very different to me now.

How do you see it going forward, and do you incorporate any of those ideas into the way you think about investing?

**Li Lu:** It’s a great question, Tano. The world has changed over the last 30 years. There’s no question about that.

The world order, or the big questions of how the world governs and operates itself, changes on a slow scale. It takes decades, sometimes a hundred years, for an order to form and transform. The current world order was founded through two great world wars, which brought the whole world to the brink of collapse. Coming out of that, an American-led order emerged. Through the Cold War and the end of the Cold War, it became one order. Now it’s transforming once again into something yet to be seen, to emerge into what would emerge out of it.

Underneath, there is another order that has been steadily forming for the last few hundred years: the economic order. It forms when more and more countries and populations join in common trade and transact business with each other. They form a common market.

That common market was finally formed roughly 30 years ago, with the collapse of the Soviet Union and the end of the Cold War. Virtually all countries and populations began to join one market economy.

What is interesting about this economic order is that, once a common market is formed, it is very difficult for any single country to withdraw voluntarily. Out of the market comes efficiency, comes the flywheel of continuous improvement along the lines of technology, efficiency, division of labor, trading of resources, and so on. Any country that withdraws for an extended period would face a great deal of cost.

In the end, once it is formed, it is very difficult to disintegrate. It lasts much longer. Look at the trade wars and trade frictions over the preceding hundred years. No matter what frictions and barriers you erect, people find a way to trade through a third country, along the same principles of comparative advantage.

Take U.S.–China trade. Of course, that has declined, but if you look at overall U.S. trade with Mexico plus ASEAN, they exactly offset it. Basically, U.S.–China trade remains the same, it just goes through a third-party country. That is roughly true in most other markets as well.

In the political order, the U.S., having led the globe for 80 years and shouldered most of the costs of ensuring that the economic common market functions as it sees fit, began to realize that the costs and benefits derived from it were probably a bit out of proportion. It began to voluntarily withdraw some of those responsibilities, not very successfully so far, leaving a vacuum.

The single biggest change in the political order is the rise of China over the last 30 or 40 years. It has become an enormous economic entity, with a complete set of manufacturing and technological self-sufficiency, and a single largest market that is also open, with unified regulation.

That is a huge change to both the political and economic orders we are witnessing. At the same time, our mutual dependency, with one single market, has only increased, not decreased, over that period.

I’ll give you one example. In generative AI, the one thing everybody needs is chips, semiconductor chips. Where are those chips being made? Think about all the countries that produce them. They have one thing in common. What do you think that might be? Sorry, I’m not putting you on the spot.

**Tano Santos:** I’m perfectly on the spot. Now I have to think about semiconductors when I was thinking that I need to open for questions. What do they all have in common?

**Li Lu:** One thing in common among all the places that produce semiconductor chips essential for this technological wave, which the whole country seems to be betting on, is that every country and all the people who produce semiconductor chips use chopsticks. Think about that. Without exception.

**Tano Santos:** [laughter]

**Li Lu:** Yes. Of course, all the people leading the semiconductor companies in this country also use chopsticks, from Jensen [Huang] to Lip-bu [Tan] to Lisa [Su], you name it, Hock [Tan], all of them.

There is a great deal of mutual dependency, as you can see. If you’re betting to the extent we’re currently betting as a country and as companies on this new technology, whatever its promise and peril could be, then you have to be nice to people who use chopsticks.

This is not changing. It didn’t happen by accident; we can go into why. There is a reason the president of the United States and the president of China find an urgency to meet four times in eight months. Two into it, the other two coming. The most important accomplishment of every meeting is that we schedule the next meeting.

That comes the term strategic stability. In other words, stability is of strategic importance. I highlighted the sense of one global common market, the cost of withdrawing voluntarily from it, and mutual dependency deepening in a peaceful manner.

Meanwhile, everybody is competing fiercely, all believing time is on their side. China to complete semiconductor self-sufficiency and all kinds of other things, the U.S. hoping for a breakthrough in AI and what it can promise. Other countries have different sets of calculations.

It all highlights that peaceful stability is of paramount importance to everyone, of strategic importance. The largest countries have the responsibility and the desire to keep it that way.

All countries would feel the weight of one single common market and the cost of withdrawing from it. The world, by and large, will continue to progress in that regard. The order we have been familiar with since the end of World War II is changing and transforming. What will emerge, and in what form, is yet to be seen. But the underlying formation of something with a more prominent impact, the forming of one single global common market, is here to stay.

What is the implication for an investor? We come back to our basic tenets of value investing, the six principles. The last one we added is that, as a global investor, your wealth is expressed as your percentage ownership of global purchasing power.

One way to accomplish that, certainly the way we’ve been practicing, is by buying great businesses in dynamic countries and markets at advantageous prices, and holding them for the long term to create value, preserve our percentage, and grow it over time.

Back to Warren’s analogy describing the stock market as a church with a casino attached to it. What Berkshire has accomplished over the last 60 years is to build that church into a gigantic cathedral, which not only symbolizes and functions, but where people can find spiritual solace, because it provides the right function for which the market exists.

What we’re trying to practice, back to your question, over the last 30 years, is that we want to prove that the gospel can be spread. We can build churches in other countries. We chose to build that church in Asia as well as other regions, as a global investor. Over time, some of those churches will appear in different countries, and some might grow into cathedrals as well.

Let’s come back to your other question, to students and young professionals: What would you choose to do in this market? This is the basic setup, a church with a casino attached as part of the permitting process because of the human condition.

They have different implications for the overall society. On one side, you have a win-win approach and a net benefit to society. On the other, you have zero-sum and a net cost, a net negative impact on society.

The choices are yours. Technology, including AI, will remain a tool, not a replacement. If it has any tendency to replace humans, as I said, we will all unite to oppose such a creation of a man-made god.

Therefore, in my view, you don’t have to participate in a prospectus that calls it an IPO of a demigod. That is one way to express our collective duties to a good society. That’s why this program exists, and I think it ought to continue. Thank you very much.
